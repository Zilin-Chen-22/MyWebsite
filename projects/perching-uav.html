Flexible Perching System for a Miniature UAV

A system-level graduation thesis on how a miniature UAV can autonomously approach an overhead target, attach through a voltage-switchable flexible interface, remain perched without continuous lift, and release on command.

From free flight to low-power residence.

Small UAVs are agile, but their endurance is fundamentally limited by the energy required to remain airborne. This work treats perching as a complete robotic transition: the vehicle must perceive a target, approach it under uncertain depth estimates, create a reliable contact, switch into an attached state, and later detach safely.

Research question and design constraints

The central question was whether a miniature quadrotor could complete this transition with a sensing and actuation stack light enough for real flight. The design target limited total mass to the load supported by the adhesive device, required at least 0.4 N of thrust margin for contact preload, and assumed an indoor environment without GPS, motion capture, or a dedicated altitude sensor. These constraints made mass distribution, visual observability, contact mechanics, and controller bandwidth parts of one coupled problem rather than separate subsystems.

Vehicle and system architecture

The core frame was machined from 1.5 mm carbon-fiber plate and weighed 8.3 g. Finite-element analysis compared a solid plate, a conventional cutout, and the final lightweight geometry; the selected structure retained micrometer-scale deformation under full-thrust loading while reducing unnecessary material. With the upward-looking camera installed, the flight platform weighed about 32.8 g; after integrating the support and drive hardware, but before adding the final VSERF material, it weighed about 48.4 g. A symmetric layout was used to reduce center-of-gravity offset and the steady disturbance torque created by the top-mounted camera and perching mechanism.

To avoid carrying a heavy onboard computer, video processing ran on a ground station. The vehicle transmitted its camera view, while OpenCV-based detection estimated the target state and returned navigation references. The onboard controller retained the fast attitude loop, so communication and vision latency affected guidance rather than basic stabilization. Full-throttle testing produced approximately 109.97 g of lift, and hover required about 43% throttle, leaving useful authority for approach and contact preload.

Voltage-switchable flexible attachment

The perching interface used voltage-switchable electrorheological fluid, or VSERF. Under an electric field, suspended particles polarize and form chain-like structures, increasing the material's effective shear strength and interface friction. The compliant surface can conform to the target during contact, while electrical switching avoids the mass and mechanical complexity of claws, pumps, or transmissions.

Bench testing showed a maximum stable adhesive force of approximately 1.12 N, equivalent to supporting about 114 g vertically. The selected operating point used roughly 2.5 kV: high enough to provide useful adhesion but below the region where electrical breakdown risk increased. A 3.3 V flight-control signal triggered the high-voltage circuit through optical isolation, separating the low-voltage controller from the actuator supply. This interface enabled three distinct states: unpowered approach, energized attachment, and commanded release after voltage removal.

Visual measurement and depth observability

The perception pipeline combined camera calibration, radial and tangential distortion correction, AprilTag corner detection, and a perspective-n-point pose solution. A 40 mm tag supplied metric scale. Image-plane offsets drove lateral alignment, while the estimated translation along the camera axis supplied the approach-distance error.

The measurements were not equally reliable in every direction. Lateral image motion produces a strong pixel displacement, so the in-plane Jacobian remains well conditioned. Depth is recovered mainly from apparent scale, whose sensitivity decays approximately with inverse squared distance. At medium range, different depths can therefore produce very similar reprojection errors. The project explicitly analyzed this conditioning problem rather than treating the pose estimate as uniformly accurate; the controller relied on vision for alignment and initial approach, then tolerated partial tag loss near contact through the faster attitude loop and vehicle inertia.

Multi-rate cascaded control

The control stack separated guidance from stabilization. A 25 Hz visual outer loop converted normalized horizontal, vertical, and depth errors into roll, pitch, and throttle references. A 50 Hz onboard controller used cascaded attitude and angular-rate PID loops to track those references and reject disturbances. This division kept computationally expensive perception offboard while preserving a faster stabilization path on the vehicle.

Controller feasibility was checked analytically as well as experimentally. The attitude-loop error dynamics were written in state-space form and evaluated with Hurwitz conditions and Lyapunov arguments. For the visual loop, a PD-dominant approximation produced second-order error dynamics; the selected gains gave positive characteristic-polynomial coefficients in all three channels, supporting asymptotic convergence under the stated time-scale assumptions.

MuJoCo task-chain simulation

The MuJoCo model combined quadrotor dynamics, the target marker, camera feedback, controller logic, contact, and the adhesive state transition. The simulated task covered takeoff, target acquisition, alignment, upward approach, partial loss of the marker at close range, actuator activation, and final attachment with rotor lift removed. This was more than a free-flight controller test: it exercised the hybrid transition between unconstrained motion, contact, and supported residence before risking the physical platform.

Physical validation and outcome

Hardware tests were staged progressively. Flights without the adhesive device first verified that the vehicle could find the tag, align, and reach the target; after contact it bounced away, confirming that navigation and attachment could be evaluated separately. Three repeated autonomous-approach trials then showed different paths under airflow and initial-condition variation, but all converged toward the target region. An assisted test confirmed that the VSERF device and high-voltage interface could support the integrated vehicle without visible slip or attitude loss.

In the complete demonstration, the UAV took off, approached the overhead target under visual servoing, made contact at approximately 3 s, activated the adhesive, and remained attached for 60 s without continuous lift. Removing the drive voltage released the interface and completed the final state transition. Together, the simulation and experiments established the engineering feasibility of a low-mass, visually guided perching system rather than only validating one isolated component.

Limitations and next steps

The current system still depends on a printed fiducial and offboard image processing, and monocular depth remains the weakest measured degree of freedom. Adhesive performance also depends on surface roughness, contact angle, material preparation, and high-voltage reliability. Future work should add more direct short-range sensing, quantify adhesion across surface classes and repeated cycles, improve terminal contact detection, and move more perception onboard while preserving the vehicle's mass margin.
